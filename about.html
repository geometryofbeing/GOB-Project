About

The Geometry of Being Project

A collective project situated at Aikido of Champlain Valley in Burlington, Vermont. The shapes belong to no one. The project belongs to the community.

The Aikido connection

Aikido is a Japanese martial art founded on a principle that sets it apart from almost every other martial tradition: the goal is not to defeat an opponent but to resolve conflict without harm — to meet force with harmony rather than opposition. Its founder, Morihei Ueshiba, described it as the art of peace.

In Aikido, circle, triangle, and square are not metaphors. They describe actual qualities of movement. The circle is the fluid, receptive turning that redirects force without meeting it head-on. The triangle is the directed, penetrating entry — decisive movement toward a specific point. The square is the stable, grounded posture that holds everything in place — the base from which all movement begins and returns.

Circle

Open · Receptive · Flowing

Receptive movement that redirects force rather than meeting it head-on.

Triangle

Directed · Decisive · Purposeful

Directed movement toward a specific point. Decisive, penetrating.

Square

Steady · Structured · Settled

The stable base from which all movement begins and returns.

A skilled practitioner moves between all three simultaneously — open enough to receive what is coming, grounded enough not to be moved, purposeful enough to know where they are going. The practice is learning to hold all three at once. Not in sequence. Not in theory. In the body, in real time, in contact with another person.

The Geometry of Being emerged from this practice. The insight at its center — that circle, triangle, and square are not opposites to be chosen between but qualities to be integrated — was not arrived at intellectually. It was felt, first, on the mat. What this project explores is what it means to carry that integration into everything else.

Contributors

Tap a photo to read about each contributor.

Benjamin Pincus is the founder and Chief Instructor of Aikido of Champlain Valley, which he opened in Burlington, Vermont in 1997. He has practiced Aikido for over 40 years and has studied with several direct students of the art’s founder. He and his wife, Heidi Albright, are co-instructors at Cloud Mountain Aikido and Living Arts in Roxbury, Vermont — a rural retreat for martial arts and integrated life skills.

He first learned of marusankakushikaku — circle, triangle, square — from Terry Dobson, who studied directly under Aikido’s founder, Morihei Ueshiba O Sensei. Dobson said he asked O Sensei only one question: what is the meaning of circle, triangle, and square in Aikido? O Sensei paused, said “Figure it out for yourself,” and walked away.

Emilie Inoue wrote the founding essay, “The Geometry of Being: Holding Shape in a Shapeless World” — the essay that led to the recognition of the WholeShape.

Emilie is Japanese and American. She spent her first fifteen years in Japan before moving to Vermont. Aikido has been an integral part of her life for the past twenty years, and an important way to stay connected to — and honor — a part of her heritage and identity. She works in state government as a public servant focused on socioecological challenges, and applies Aikido’s principles off the mat, in daily life and in her work. What draws her most is Aikido’s attention to the space in between — which is exactly where connection happens.

Fletcher Pratt is in large part a metal fabricator, which led him into the world of 3D modeling and printing. He modeled the WholeShape based on a discussion about the “The Geometry of Being: Holding Shape in a Shapeless World” essay. Something that had been described in words became something you could hold.

Heidi Albright has studied Aikido for over 30 years. She is also an artist, craftsperson, designer, scientist, and farmer. Her work is inspired by nature and how it holds all shapes at once, always shifting toward balance.

She and Pincus Sensei are co-instructors at Cloud Mountain Living Arts and Aikido, a country dojo and retreat location in central Vermont.

Michael Trimpol made the WholeShape in glass — a one-of-a-kind contemplative offering that holds the form up to light.

Michael has spent his life in two pursuits: earning his living as a maker — first in stained glass, then as a glassblower — and practicing the martial arts of Judo and Aikido. Both shaped how he perceives the world. Glass can be liquid at one moment and solid the next, and often transparent — letting you see all of its dimensions at once. The martial arts ask for the same doubleness: grounded and fluid at the same time.
